# Plain-language writing standard

Read and apply this guide whenever drafting or revising copy in this project, including Confluence application pages, the portfolio, case studies, Drawing Board articles, résumés, and cover letters. Use it with the `avidano-writing` skill and the current approved sources.

Reviewed September 27, 2026: Digital.gov's [plain-language principles](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles) and the linked sections on writing, design, and testing. The guidance below is paraphrased and adapted for this project. The application examples and review checklist are our working practices.

## Start with the reader

Before writing, establish who is reading, what they already know, and what they need to understand or do. Use the job description, current source material, and Carl's instructions to answer those questions. Give different audiences clear paths to the information relevant to them. [Write for the reader](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/write-for-reader).

Choose the level of detail for that audience. Plain language does not require an arbitrary school reading level or the removal of useful technical detail. A recruiter may need a brief explanation of a component's purpose; a design-system specialist may need its behavior and constraints. Make the shared explanation understandable to both. [Principles overview](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles).

## Put the main point where readers can find it

Lead with the purpose, relevant finding, or decision. Add supporting detail in the order readers need it. Put instructions in the order someone performs them. Introduce the usual situation before exceptions, while keeping any condition that changes the meaning close to the claim. Move secondary background later. Longer material may benefit from a short opening summary or links to sections. [Organize the information](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/organize).

For this portfolio, make the connection to the role clear early. In a case study, explain the problem and Carl's contribution before a detailed account of the process. Preserve approved sections and their order; a plain-language pass does not authorize redesigning an application page or adding summary boxes to Drawing Board articles.

## Make the person and action clear

Prefer active sentences that identify who did what. Use direct verbs instead of phrases that turn actions into abstract nouns. Present tense suits current behavior and instructions; past tense accurately describes completed work. Passive voice can be useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, but must not conceal responsibility. [Writing for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing).

Give each sentence a clear main idea and each paragraph one topic. Split sentences that bury their point in qualifications. Keep related conditions connected to the statement they qualify. Vary sentence and paragraph length naturally; clarity does not require a sequence of equally short sentences. [Clear and short](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short).

These illustrative edits show the approach; they are not claims about Carl's projects:

| Wordy or unclear | Clearer |
| --- | --- |
| A review of the navigation was conducted by the team. | The team reviewed the navigation. |
| The purpose of this page is to provide an explanation of the settings. | This page explains the settings. |
| Component documentation maintenance workflow | How we maintain component documentation |
| It is not necessary to create an account to read the articles. | You can read the articles without an account. |

The last two edits also apply Digital.gov's advice on noun strings and negative constructions. Preserve the actual meaning when making a similar edit. [Style](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/style).

## Choose familiar, precise words

Use ordinary words when they carry the intended meaning: “use” instead of “utilize,” for example. In guidance, distinguish requirements from recommendations: “must” states a requirement, “should” a recommendation, and “may” permission. [Familiar terms](https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms).

Keep necessary technical terms and explain unfamiliar ones where they first appear. Remove language that makes a claim sound impressive without explaining the work. Do not force readers through a glossary before they can understand the opening. [Avoid jargon](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/avoid-jargon).

Use one term consistently for the same concept. Cut repeated ideas, empty modifiers, and introductions that postpone the point. Keep qualifications that establish scope, uncertainty, or attribution. [Short and simple](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/short-simple).

Reduce unfamiliar acronyms; a short descriptive name often works better. Explain acronyms the audience needs, without defining familiar terms unnecessarily. Unpack strings of nouns, resolve ambiguous “and/or,” and make the relationship between paragraphs clear through their content. Use examples when they explain a difficult idea more directly. [Style](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/style).

## Use structure to support understanding

Write concise headings that tell readers what a section contains. Use meaningful heading levels and avoid unnecessary nesting. A question heading can help when it matches a real reader question; do not invent questions to force every section into a question-and-answer format. Preserve approved project names and section headings. [Headings](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/headings).

Use lists for genuinely parallel items and numbered lists for ordered steps. Introduce each list and keep the items grammatically consistent. Use tables for comparisons or relationships that are difficult to follow in prose. Avoid turning the whole page into bullets. [Lists](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/lists), [Tables](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/tables).

Use space and occasional emphasis to make important information visible. Avoid all-capital passages and underlining that looks like a link. Choose figures that help explain the point and retain their accessible text alternatives; a screenshot should not carry an essential explanation by itself. [Design for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design), [Visuals](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/visuals).

## Make links and downloads understandable

For inline links to external websites, use the existing `LinkOpenNew` component. It supplies the new-window icon, opens the destination in a new tab, and includes the screen-reader announcement. Do this when adding the link; do not insert a plain Markdown link and leave the component conversion for later. Preserve the approved link text. Hemingway text exports remain plain text.

Link text should identify the destination or action. Avoid vague labels such as “click here.” Keep essential context on the page instead of sending readers through repeated cross-references. Identify downloadable files by type and include useful, verified details such as size or version date. [Links](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design/links).

Explain the main content on the web page. A résumé PDF can provide a downloadable document, while the page still explains relevant experience. Add an FAQ only when real reader questions justify it; do not use one to patch an unclear main page or duplicate its content. [Special cases](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/special-cases).

When Carl supplies revised copy, check factual accuracy and grammar before saving it. Preserve his paragraph breaks as well as his short sentences and intended attribution. Correct ambiguous pronouns and inconsistent tense without inventing a cause, result, or actor. Carl explicitly reaffirmed these requirements during the UNICEF review on October 3, 2026.

During a wording discussion, return the requested text in chat. Approval of a passage alone is not a request to synchronize files or Confluence; wait for an explicit request to update or save. Carl clarified this on October 4 during the LADRC review, then explicitly authorized applying his complete revision. Respect his direct confirmation of delivered work rather than limiting an outcome to the artifacts available in the archive.

Keep concise writing proportionate to the actual work. On October 4, Carl emphasized that his portfolio must stop underselling his contribution. Name the verified platform changes, authoring capabilities, organizational scope, and design responsibility; do not compress them into a generic website-redesign summary. For CCF, the central work includes WordPress Multisite, ACF Flexible Content modules reused across pages and templates, staff publishing tools, and the visitor experience for a global organization. Accuracy includes stating the full supported scope, with appropriate individual and team attribution.

These are project-specific applications of the principles, alongside Jared Spool's guidance and Carl's existing editorial requirements:

| Content | What the reader should understand |
| --- | --- |
| Application introduction | Why Carl's verified experience is relevant to this particular role. |
| Case study | The problem, Carl's responsibility, consequential decisions, and supported results. Keep individual contributions distinct from team outcomes. |
| Drawing Board article | One useful decision, experiment, or lesson, with enough context to understand it. Preserve its informal voice and approved title. |
| Résumé or cover letter | Specific work and relevant experience, using accurate titles, dates, and employer terminology. Avoid generic capability claims. |
| Caption or image description | What the figure shows and the context needed to interpret it. Avoid claiming results that the image cannot establish. |

Keep Carl's warm, direct voice. Preserve approved facts, names, chronology, and meaningful limitations. Shortening a sentence must not turn a possibility into an outcome, hide an evidence gap, or imply testing that did not occur.

## Review and test understanding

Review every draft against these questions:

1. Can the intended reader identify the point and its relevance from the opening?
2. Is the order useful, with clear headings and link labels?
3. Is it clear who acted, what they did, and which claims the evidence supports?
4. Can unfamiliar terms, noun strings, repetition, or unnecessary modifiers be removed or explained?
5. Does each paragraph add useful information while preserving necessary nuance?
6. Does the copy sound natural when read aloud, without forced rhythm, slogans, or a generic closing summary?

For important or widely reused material, consider reader testing while the copy is still a draft. In paraphrase testing, ask intended readers to explain a section in their own words and describe what they would do next. Listen without correcting or leading them. A yes-or-no question about whether it is clear gives little evidence. [Paraphrase testing](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/paraphrase-testing).

When finding information is part of the task, observe readers using the whole page or document. Revise where they struggle, then check again. Comparative testing can measure differences between versions but does not explain the causes by itself. [Usability testing](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/usability-testing), [Comparative studies](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test/comparative-studies).

Scale testing to the material and the audience; a separate study is not a prerequisite for every application draft. An editorial review, readability score, or AI assessment is not evidence that readers understood the text. Record actual testing only when it has been performed.
