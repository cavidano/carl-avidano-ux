## Central idea

Build the portfolio around evidence that you can do the work required by a particular position. A reader should be able to recognize relevant experience without needing you there to explain the connection. The landing page establishes the fit, and the case studies substantiate it. (Pages 9-11, 14.)

## Additional source read October 6: comparable experience

Carl supplied [What’s Your Comparable Experience?](Whats_Your_Comparable_Experience.pdf), a four-page Jared Spool / Center Centre handout from *The Essentials of Finding Your Next UX Job*, episode 1. All four pages were read and visually reviewed. Use it alongside the workshop below.

- Pages 2–3 define comparable experience through the specific work an employer needs done, rather than a generic job title. Make the connection between that work and verified past experience easy to recognize.
- Page 3 treats finding that evidence as a usability problem: the hiring manager should not have to work to uncover the match. Coordinate the portfolio, résumé, and any cover letter around the actual posting.
- For leadership roles, page 3 specifically asks what the candidate’s past teams accomplished that compares with what the hiring manager needs to accomplish. Show supported team results and Carl’s contribution to them, alongside individual craft.
- Page 4 supports using multiple tailored résumés or portfolios when that makes the connection clearer.

The handout supports the concept of comparable experience; it does not prescribe that exact phrase as a section heading. Carl introduced it after discussing “Comparable experience” versus the approved “Relevant experience” application heading. Do not dismiss “comparable” as inherently defensive or assume it requires the same industry. No public copy change was requested by the request to read this source.

## October 8 source: application packet and achiever pattern

Carl added [Section 2: Creating Your Application Packet — Part 1](Section_2_Creating_Your_Application_Packet_-_Part_1.pdf), a nine-page handout from Jared Spool’s UX job-search course. All nine pages were read on October 8; the relevant pages 6–7 were also rendered and visually reviewed. The original PDF remains local and outside the website release.

- Pages 2–5 treat the application packet (résumé, portfolio, LinkedIn, and interactions) as a way to communicate an authentic, coherent account of the candidate’s relevant experience.
- Page 6 distinguishes experience and the judgment learned through work from a list of skills or a description of a generic process. Show what the candidate learned, including what did not work, and how that informs future work.
- Pages 6–7 define the achiever pattern as growth over a career: increasingly advanced work, responsibility, reach, influence, challenges, and accomplishments. Years served and changing job titles alone do not establish it.
- Page 7 asks how lessons from earlier projects informed later ones and what value the work created: who benefited and what would have been missing without the candidate’s contribution. Quantified outcomes can help, but scope, judgment, initiative, and the value of completed work are also relevant evidence.
- Page 8 recommends selecting the experience that matters for the target role and avoiding personal details that distract from that connection. Carl’s disability statement is directly connected to inclusive design; do not treat it as an unrelated hobby or automatically remove it.

For future résumé and portfolio reviews, assess both the progression visible across roles and the concrete evidence within each role. Keep the distinction between showing broad experience and showing growth in responsibility and judgment. Avoid inventing promotions, continuous year-by-year advancement, team sizes, metrics, causal claims, or lessons not supplied by Carl.

## Guidance from the workshop

| Principle | What it means for a portfolio | PDF pages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Make comparable experience explicit. | Connect previous work to the responsibilities and problems in the job description. General capability alone may leave the reader uncertain about fit. | 7-9 |
| Tailor for one position at a time. | Adjust the emphasis and selected evidence for the role. Jared notes that much of the underlying material may remain the same. | 9-10, 14 |
| Let the portfolio answer interview questions. | Anticipate what an employer needs to know about your qualifications and provide those answers in the site. | 9-10 |
| Keep the landing page focused. | Make the relevant qualifications easy to recognize. A comprehensive career retrospective can obscure the evidence a particular employer needs. | 10-11 |
| Use case studies to establish fit. | Focus on work that addresses the job requirements rather than recounting every stage of a long project. | 11-14 |
| Show contributions and judgment. | Explain what you contributed, the decisions you made, and how those decisions led to results. Generic descriptions of a standard UX process make candidates difficult to distinguish. | 13 |

Jared describes a screening model with yes, no, and maybe groups. His warning is that unclear evidence can leave a qualified applicant in the maybe group, which hiring managers may never revisit. The practical takeaway is to make the connection between experience and job requirements explicit. (Pages 8-10.)

The workshop also presents Jared's account of changes in the UX job market since late 2022. Treat those statements as the speaker's dated assessment, not as independently verified facts or universal rules about every employer. (Pages 6-8.)

## How we will apply this guide

These are project-specific working practices derived from the workshop, not additional claims attributed to Jared.

1. Start with the actual job description and the work the employer needs done.
2. Map each important requirement to verified experience, a case study, or a relevant Drawing Board article.
3. Identify missing evidence before rewriting. Ask Carl for specifics where his responsibilities, decisions, or outcomes are unclear.
4. Put the strongest relevant examples first and make Carl's role explicit.
5. Review each case study for the problem, Carl's contribution, consequential decisions, and supported results. Retain process details and figures when they help establish those points.
6. Check whether the page answers likely hiring questions without a spoken explanation.
7. Preserve approved facts and distinguish individual contributions from team outcomes. Do not invent metrics, motivations, or results to make a stronger hiring story.

A useful mapping for a future application:

| Job requirement | Verified example | Carl's contribution or decision | Evidence to show | Missing information |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Add the employer's actual requirement | Identify the relevant project | Describe the specific responsibility | Link the supporting passage or figure | Record what needs clarification |

## Questions to use during a review

- What work would this portfolio give someone confidence to hire Carl to do?
- Where is the connection to the target role immediately clear, and where must the reader infer it?
- Are we showing Carl's judgment and contribution, or only naming activities and deliverables?
- Which details help establish relevant experience? Which can be shortened or removed?
- What evidence is still needed to support the claims?

This guide informs future decisions. It does not by itself call for changing the current public site, creating a separate site for every role, or removing approved case-study content. Continue to use the current Confluence copy and Carl's latest instructions for editorial decisions.
