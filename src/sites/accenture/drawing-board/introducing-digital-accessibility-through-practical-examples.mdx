## What we covered

We introduced digital accessibility through common disabilities and barriers, the four principles—perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust—and a brief overview of accessibility laws.

The practical examples focused on eight areas:

- **Programmatic access:** Structuring content with semantic HTML and built-in styles, and using tables for data.
- **Images and labels:** Describing meaningful images with alt text and clearly labeling icons, buttons, links, and form fields.
- **Keyboard access:** Making interactive elements usable without a mouse, with visible focus and a clear navigation order.
- **Fonts and typography:** Using readable type, spacing, and hierarchy, and keeping important text out of images.
- **Color and contrast:** Keeping text and controls easy to see without relying on color alone to convey information.
- **Motion and playback:** Giving people ways to pause moving content, control playback, and reduce unnecessary motion.
- **Accessible multimedia:** Providing captions, transcripts, and audio description to make video and audio available to more people.
- **Plain language:** Writing clearly so people can find what they need and understand it the first time.

We also conducted a light accessibility review of the nonprofit’s website and shared practical recommendations to help their team plan improvements.

## Making the presentation itself accessible

Accessibility also shaped the presentation itself. We used clear headings and plain language to organize the explanations. In InDesign, we organized the slide content in the Articles panel to define the intended reading order. The PDF includes document tags, a defined language, and bookmarks—structural features that support navigation and interpretation by assistive technology alongside the visual design.
