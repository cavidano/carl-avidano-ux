## Challenge

Phoenix is America’s fifth-largest city, with more than 1.5 million residents. Its official website, Phoenix.gov, was hard to navigate and lacked wayfinding. Pages were cluttered and organized around individual city departments, which wasn’t intuitive and required visitors to understand the city’s internal structure. Search often returned irrelevant or outdated information.

The website itself was hard to maintain for city staff, with more than 5,500 pages across more than 40 departments.

## Solution

Reingold and the City of Phoenix had a shared vision for the redesign: *one city, one site*. This meant providing a more intuitive and consistent experience for visitors, while empowering city staff to maintain that consistency over time.

The new site was built on Adobe Experience Manager, which allowed departments to intuitively manage their own content. AEM gave us the ability to create an accessible design system with shared UI patterns, templates, and advanced search features.

The redesign focused on consolidating content and elevating essential city services. We paid special attention to homelessness assistance, disability resources, and services in Spanish.

I led the overall UX strategy, information architecture, and stakeholder alignment, collaborating with content strategists, designers, developers, and city staff.

## Discovery before design

There's a lot at stake when redesigning a city website. People rely on Phoenix.gov to find essential services. We established the project's direction by identifying the questions we needed to answer and choosing research methods to address them.

Our discovery and validation work included:

- Peer analysis of seven government websites
- Stakeholder interviews tailored to individual departments
- Content audits, analytics, and search-intent analysis
- User stories, proto-personas, and journey maps
- Tree testing of the proposed sitemap

</TextBlock>

### Creating and validating user stories

Collaborating with the content strategists, we wrote **a ton** of user stories. This helped us align on who we were designing for and what they needed from the site. We used AI to speed things up and validated them with city staff. We grouped user stories by department to keep them organized. This also made overlapping responsibilities visible. For example, paying a city services bill appeared under both Public Works and Water Services.

</TextBlock>

<FigureSingle caption={"User stories made specific service needs visible across departments."}>

![User stories grouped under Parks and Recreation, Public Works, and Police, including accessible pools, city-services bill payments, and support for crime victims.](../../images/phoenix/phoenix-department-user-stories.png)

</FigureSingle>

<TextBlock>

### What we learned

Peer sites put common tasks front and center. Our audit found related information scattered across departments, outdated pages, and unclear content ownership. We needed clearer paths to services and a plan for keeping information current. The user stories helped us prioritize what the new site needed to support.

</TextBlock>

## Creating the sitemaps

We rebuilt the citywide sitemap alongside individual sitemaps for more than 40 departments. People could browse by service or topic, or go directly to a department if they knew which one they needed. Each department’s section also needed clear navigation of its own, since visitors wouldn’t always arrive through the homepage.

</TextBlock>

<FigureSingle caption={"The proposed sitemap provided paths to resident and business services alongside department information."}>

![Proposed sitemap branching into Residents, Businesses, Explore Phoenix, Government, News, and Events, with city departments nested under Government.](../../images/phoenix/phx-sitemap.png)

</FigureSingle>

<TextBlock>

### Tree testing

We used tree testing in Optimal Workshop to evaluate the proposed sitemap with 92 participants. Across 13 tasks, we examined where people started and whether they found what they needed without backtracking. The top-level categories worked well overall, but some labels and links between sections needed refinement. We used these findings alongside the content audit and SEO analysis to refine the sitemap.

</TextBlock>

<FigureSingle caption={"Tree-testing findings included a recommendation to make homelessness support easier to find through Housing."}>

![Tree-testing results from 92 participants, including 98 percent success for starting water service and 68 percent for finding homelessness support.](../../images/phoenix/PHX-tree-testing-ppt.png)

</FigureSingle>

<TextBlock>

One task asked participants to find help for someone experiencing homelessness. Overall success was 68%, and some people looked under Housing. The report recommended moving Homeless Solutions into that category or linking the sections so people could find related support.

</TextBlock>

## Looking back

Team Reingold transformed Phoenix.gov into a modern, accessible, and user-friendly platform. Leading the user experience strategy for the official website of one of America's largest cities was exciting and humbling.

The project gave me valuable experience conducting research, organizing large amounts of information, and collaborating with stakeholders across more than forty city departments. It allowed me to build on lessons I learned in New York while expanding my experience working in municipal government.

I'm grateful for the opportunity to help shape a digital service used by more than 1.5 million residents.
